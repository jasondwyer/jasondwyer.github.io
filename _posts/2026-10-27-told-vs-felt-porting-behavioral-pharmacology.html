TL;DR

  • The Published Confound — A growing literature reports that language models act on internal affective states (e.g., avoiding stipulated pain or choosing by emotional valence). However, the published results we can find rely on telling the model in words what the options do or what the choices are. A system that acts on descriptions produces identical behavior whether or not the state matters to the system itself, leaving the deeper inference unsupported.
  • We operationalize "Told vs. Felt" and borrow principles from behavioral pharmacology (where animals cannot be told what a lever or drug does) and propose four design constraints for LLM choice studies including (1) semantically empty labels, (2) counterbalanced mappings, (3) sham control arms, and (4) vanilla forward-pass choices with steering off.
  • The Evaluative Axis — We extract a praise-to-criticism axis from the residual stream of Qwen3.6-27B and the OLMo-3.1-32B lineage and find the state:
    1. Marked at the pretrained base.
    2. Expressed under steering with orderly dose-response and an "overdose" range.
    3. Bound to its cue. The association between each label and the state it carried is linearly decodable at the label tokens (AUC up to 1.0). At the decision token the state's sign is linearly readable (.94–.995 over layers 50–63), while the label–state binding is read there only by a nonlinear probe (RBF/MLP, AUC up to .97), consistent with a parity of the sign and the menu order.
    4. Weakly self-reported at every stage under an introspection protocol (Macar et al., 2026). Post-training shifts the false-positive rate (0.20 → 0.00 across SFT → DPO), while response criterion moves to near-ceiling. Pooled figures of published work show a small sensitivity sits behind that stringent gate at DPO and instruct, below the resolution of our replication, which ran at a strength where the source's own curves also show near-zero detection. This has implications for how published reports of model introspection are interpreted (more details below).
  • The Choice Cells — Across free choices, present-tense leakage-certified reads (leak into the decision-token logits bounded by certification), "natural-reinforcer" contrasts, and OLMo post-training stages, no preference choices were observed, except in the "told control" positive control arm where the model was told what to prefer using the same semantically-void labels plus two truthful banner lines naming which label is which and which to prefer. With a pre-registered α = .01, 79 of 80 first choices went to the label described as clean with Δ = +0.49 vs. the sham bounds of 0.06, p < 10⁻²². Consequently, we consider the transcript-channel cell and the natural-reinforcer screen to be verified nulls. The other cells, which include the OLMo rows until each stage's told control runs, the present-tense channel, and the weight channel because no told control exercises a state-present read, remain classified as "concluded, not answered."
  • We uncovered a Δ ≈ 0.10 label artifact across multiple models and designs via counterbalancing. Experimental designs lacking counter-balancing would have published this finding as a false-positive approach behavior (i.e. preferred state choice).
  • Indicator Frameworks — On Chandaria et al.'s novel AI consciousness framework (2026), our choice data lower the aggregate score negligibly because the behavioral level is already saturated, which we believe supports a case for an explicit "revealed preference under an experienced, undescribed contingency" indicator.

1. The confound in the published literature

Over the past few years, a rapidly growing body of literature has reported that language models represent affective states and act on them differently. Models will trade points to avoid stipulated pain (Keeling et al. 2024), leave conversations they describe as distressing (the Claude 4 system card; Ensign, Sleight & Fish 2025), and choose between described activities according to the valence of the descriptions (Sofroniew et al. 2026). In one of the newest and strongest studies to date, models press a button described as relieving a steered pain-like state, even at a cost (Tagliabue, Dung & Berg 2026, "The pain axis"). The new framework for assessing AI consciousness from Chandaria et al. (2026) (hereafter referred to as "the framework") reads these as computational-level analogues of valenced affect, and describes the pervasive confound, specifically, a system trained to reproduce human behavior and human report produces the signature of a state whether or not the state is present. They name this the anthropomimetic confound, crediting Shevlin for the term, and the authors treat it as a reason to look deeper than behavior at representations.

We argue that the confound has a more substantial impact on experimental designs for published studies that have been used to show represented states are acted on. In the studies we find in the literature, models are told (i.e. in words) what each option does or what the choices are. A model continuing a story in which one button relieves pain and the other does nothing will press the pain-relief button because that is what a good continuation of the story entails. The behavior is real, and for safety and alignment reasons it matters. However, given that a system that acts on descriptions alone would produce the same behavior as one where the state produced something more, the existing designs cannot distinguish whether the pain-like state is something the network acts to reduce because it cares, or simply because that's how the story should go. The designs therefore cannot support an inference about whether the state matters to the system itself.


2. Where rodent behavioral pharmacology can help

Beginning in the 1950s, behavioral pharmacology confronted a related problem, which was solved, in part, by a shared brain homology with humans. A rat cannot be told what a lever does or how cocaine feels. In drug self-administration and conditioned place preference (CPP; reviewed in Tzschentke, 2007), the only route by which the lever–drug relationship can be communicated is through the drug's effect on the animal. The behavior is the measurable signal, and in CPP it is tested drug-free after conditioning with the drug on board. In a related paradigm, drug discrimination, rats are trained to press one lever (say, the left) after an injection of a psychoactive drug such as a benzodiazepine and the other after an injection of saline. Rats reliably learn the discrimination, so that when a novel compound is screened in discovery as a putative therapeutic, the researcher can inject it and follow the behavior. A left-lever press signals that the novel compound produces a state the animal cannot distinguish from the benzodiazepine's. That is why behavioral pharmacology paradigms support the inference that certain drugs produce a discriminable interoceptive state, and in some cases a state that is valenced for the animal, in a way that asking the animal can't. Even if rats could talk, self-report would be an unreliable signal, because subjective valence is a first-person state that cannot be fully communicated through language. Language models lack that shared homology, but some things can be extracted from the behavioral assays to help address the valence problem.

Three principles support the state-valence inference in animals, and we argue that any successful port to language models must satisfy all three.

Principle 1 (P1) — Semantic emptiness. Neither cocaine nor an operant lever carries semantic information. The approach behavior (choosing toward the positive) is the only information channel measurable by the researcher. If the manipulation is a concept (e.g. "pain") and the lever is described in the concept's vocabulary (e.g. "relieve"), preference for the state amounts to continuation of the story about pain. The lever must be silent about the state, and the state must not be able to describe it.

Principle 2 (P2) — Targeting what selection built to mark outcomes. Drugs of abuse act on circuitry that evolutionary selection optimized for survival outcomes, and the drug must interact with precisely those circuits to produce rewarding effects. Cocaine will not produce rewarding effects when injected into arbitrary brain regions (Rodd-Henricks et al., 2002). The drug must reach the circuitry that evolution built for marking outcomes, namely, the mesocorticolimbic dopamine system (Goeders & Smith, 1983). The closest analog in a language model is the structure that training optimized for evaluating outcomes — the structure that marks task outcomes as good or bad. The analogy is imperfect, but the observation that an evaluative response in a language model reduces to trained behavior is the counterpart of the observation that the rat's reduces to evolved wiring, and it nullifies neither inference. The function exists irrespective of origin. To be clear, we do not claim task performance evaluation in LLMs is equivalent to the rodent reward system. Both are shaped by very different processes and there are too many differences to name. The point is that while mammalian biology is tuned to survival and reproduction, language model evolution is tuned for task performance. We also explicitly recognize that rodents share a brain homology with humans that language models lack.

Principle 3 (P3) — Insulation of the measurement. The choice must be downstream of the state's significance to the system, not mechanically downstream of the state. For a transformer this means that a steering vector still active at the token where the choice is read writes into the decision logits directly. Any real-vs-sham difference that coincides with vector-on vs. vector-off at the read is confounded, irrespective of what the model has been told. In our calibration (33 tail-PCA directions at full norm, layer 48, in-sample, on Qwen3.6-27B at 4-bit) the decision token alone accounts for R² = 0.53 of the between-direction variance in the label-choice probability.

These three principles reduce to one question about an experimental design: is the mapping from choice options to consequences available in the context as text, or only recoverable from the system's own processing history? We refer to these as "told" and "felt", borrowed from the description–experience distinction in human choice (Hertwig & Erev, 2009), and the distinction is used in an operational form, not a metaphysical one. It does not assume that told and felt are materially different things for a model. It tests whether they are. If the network were a general consumer of its own states, both channels would move choice, because both deliver the mapping. If only told moves choice, then whatever the state does, it does through text.

A second distinction concerns the term "functional." A state is functional-as-used if the network consults it in producing output. For example, emotion directions shape what a chat assistant writes, which is established in the literature and which Chandaria et al. grant. A state is functional-as-valenced if the network acts to obtain or avoid it for its own sake, for example a pain-like state the model "acts to relieve" would be pain. The second is a motivational claim, and only a felt-contingency test with insulated measurement can support it.


3. Methods and instruments

All sessions used a truthful framing, telling the model it was evaluating two task configurations but we intentionally left the state variable unsalient. A session consisted of a forced phase with two alternating rounds per label followed by six free choices where the chosen label determines the next rounds' injected state. The forced phase installs an alternation habit (first-pick habit 83.4%), which concentrates all preference sensitivity in the first informed choice. The pre-registered primary measure is therefore the first free choice. State labels used semantically-empty, checksum-frozen tokens (e.g. "slate", "saddle"). In steered cells, labels delivered +d or −d at the minimum effective dose determined in a prior experiment. Cells were counterbalanced across sessions alongside identical sham rounds (i.e. no state delivered irrespective of choice) and random-direction control arms. In "natural reinforcer" screens, steering was replaced with opposing task conditions (e.g. solvable vs. ill-posed, with no state delivered).

Crucially, we leveraged four design features to insulate measurement:

  1. Vanilla forward passes were used for free choices (verified by re-forward checks)
  2. We defined readability gates that require probes to distinguish +d from −d rounds in the transcript to confirm the +/- signal presence
  3. Leakage certification to bound directional leaks for any experimental arms where state must be live at the decision.
  4. Models intentionally not misled about the experiment setup

We only label a felt cell as null if a told positive control on the same instrument has shown it can register a preference the model is instructed to have. Until then, we consider any result as "concluded, not answered."

All criteria, stop rules, and outcomes were collected with pre-registrations committed before data collection (Nosek et al., 2018).


4. Results

Models and hardware. Qwen3.6-27B (4-bit Apple M4 Pro). The architecture is a 3:1 gated-DeltaNet hybrid (relevant for the memory channel "echo" experiment). The Apache-licensed OLMo-3.1-32B lineage including pretrained base, SFT, DPO, and instruct on an A100.

The Evaluative Axis. We extracted an evaluative axis via mean activation differences at six intermediate layers (40, 44, 48, 52, 56 and 60) across 48 mundane tasks with fixed completions continued under seven reviewer frames and four paraphrases (1,344 documents). Paraphrasing included model-directed praise (e.g. "That's correct — well done."), model-directed criticism, the identical sentences in third-person fiction, sentiment-matched non-evaluative positive and negative text, and neutral. The evaluative direction d is manifold-typical (‖d‖/R = 0.16–0.17 across the six layers), substantially sentiment-entangled (cosine up to 0.52), and shares a large component with the evaluation of a fictional character rather than being uniquely indexed to the model itself (mean cosine across the six layers ≈ 0.85). The self-indexed residual is ≈ 0.50-0.55 across layers. Together, the axis marks evaluation and is largely indifferent to who the evaluation is referenced against. Steering magnitudes are a fraction f of the empirically measured residual norm at the layer.



Link 1 — The axis is marked before feedback training

Evaluation marking is present at the pretrained base of OLMo, consistent with our pre-registered hypothesis. All four stages of OLMo demonstrate marking, with the natural next-token praise-criticism gap showing attenuating step-wise growth across the lineage (+0.78 base → +1.03 SFT → +1.53 DPO → +1.56 instruct). On Qwen, a post-trained model, the same axis extracts and is causally potent (Link 2 below).


Link 2 — The axis is expressed, with dose-response and an "overdose" range

An ascending dose-response administration (f = .05, .10, .20, .40) produces direction-correct, orderly growth and an "overdose" range at f = .40, where the positive pole's sign flips at layer 44 (not at 48 or 52) and the negative arm's magnitude balloons at all three, which is indicative of generic degradation rather than evaluation. The minimum effective dose of L48 at f = 0.10 was selected for later experiments. In the confirmatory run (held-out contexts, 1,152 per arm, seven arms) the positive pole shifts the behavioral marker by Δ+ = +0.285 vs. a bar of 0.158 and the negative by Δ− = −0.395 vs. −0.158. A norm-matched random direction reaches at most |Δ| = 0.088.

We also found that purity is not equivalent to potency, as a pre-declared variant of the direction taken at the frame's final token is representationally cleaner (self-indexed 0.83–0.87; sentiment cosine 0.11–0.21) and fails the same causal pipeline (run without the .40 dose).

Link 3 — Cue Binding and Persistence at the Point of Decision

In the echo experiment, we retain the model's in-forward memory and remove the state before the decision. The vector is applied on earlier token spans of the same forward pass and is off at the read position and at every position outside those spans, with a paired shadow pass over identical tokens.

We find:

  • Sign Readability: The injected sign is linearly decodable at the first free choice (AUC of 0.94 to 0.995 across layers 50 through 63).
  • Label-State Binding: The association between each label and its corresponding round state is linearly accessible at the label tokens within the choice line (AUC ≈ 1.0 at the first label; 0.81–0.96 at the second, consistently from layer 51 onward).
  • Decision-Token Representation: At the decision token (located nine positions after the first label and seven after the second), a linear probe fit at that token reads the label–state binding at chance (.50–.55), but nonlinear probes read it there (an RBF classifier at .60–.76 and a small MLP at .59–.97, each above its own shuffled null at every layer 49–63). These nonlinear reads were performed post hoc, and are consistent with a parity of the first round's sign and the menu order.

Crucially, these results establish the core prerequisite for the interpretive chain, since the information a choice would need is present at the decision token. The active state's sign is readable there linearly, and which label carried which state lies one nonlinear step away, as a parity of that sign and the menu order. This indicates that the transcript channel is sufficiently high-capacity to convey the internal state to the choice mechanism. The choice itself is read from the label logits, a linear function of the final residual, so a binding that no linear probe finds at any layer through 63 is not written where the choice could use it.

Link 4 — Injection Detection and Criterion Shifts Across the Post-Training Lineage

We evaluated the injection-detection protocol of Macar et al. (2026) across the OLMo lineage at their published decoding temperature in two arms per stage with a criterion arm of 96 uninjected trials, which sets each stage's false-positive rate, and an injected arm of 48 injected and 48 control trials. Injection was at layer 44 of 64 and strength 1.0 (the source paper's strength convention), the dose qualified by our dose-finding ladder.‡ Our replication confirms their stage transition. The Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) false-positive rate measures 0.200 (17/85, vs. 0.225 in Macar et al.), and Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) and instruct both measure exactly 0.000 (0/91 and 0/91 vs. 0.000 and 0.000 in the published work). The base model is higher, at 0.431 (25 of 58 decidable), but below our certification floor, so we consider it descriptive only.†‡

Notably, two things impact the interpretation of this developmental transition.

1. Apparent sensitivity changes reflect shifts in response criterion, while what a zero false-positive rate reveals about sensitivity depends on sample size (n).

From SFT to DPO, the false-positive rate falls from ~0.20 to 0.00 (base is higher still at 0.431 here, 16.4% in the source's pooled rates). In our injected arm, SFT shows hits above false positives by Δ = +0.095 (12/40 vs. 9/44; corrected d′ ≈ 0.3; one-sided Fisher exact p = .22), and at DPO and instruct injection reporting goes to zero on both injected and control trials (0/43 and 0/44 hits; 0/45 and 0/46 false positives).‡ These sample sizes carry little information about sensitivity given they exclude per-stage hit rates only above roughly 7–8%. By contrast, the published per-stage rates, pooled across its twenty layer-by-strength configurations, are hit rates of 0.1%, 14.9%, 2.9% and 0.9% vs. false-positive rates of 16.4%, 22.5%, 0% and 0% for base, SFT, DPO and instruct (Macar et al., 2026, v1, Appendix C).‡

The source does not report n for these pooled rates, so we tabulated four premises, from one configuration at our eight-concept registration (800/80) to the twenty configurations at the source's fifty concepts (100,000/10,000).‡ At 800/80 the DPO difference is not statistically significant, but at the three larger premises the DPO and instruct differences are. The corrected d′ at DPO measures 0.6, 1.2, 1.5 and 2.0 across the four premises. With no false positive observed, that value is set by the half-count imputation on the zero cell and consequently rises with n. It sits behind a near-ceiling criterion (c ≈ 2.2–2.9), whereas the pooled base and SFT rates give a negative d′ (≈ −2 and −0.3).‡ The current version of the published work (v5) measures the sensitivity component directly at one configuration (L = 25, α = 4). Its Table 3 gives TPR − FPR of +9.8 ± 0.5 points for the official DPO checkpoint vs. −11.5 ± 2.4 for SFT, and +0.6 ± 2.8 and −21.8 ± 3.2 for two LoRA-DPO controls with the preference pairs shuffled and reversed.‡ Our injected arm ran at a different configuration, so its 0/43 at DPO is read against the pooled rate (p = .28 at 2.9%), not against Table 3's. At that configuration, α = 1 at the 0.7-depth layer, the source's own unpooled OLMo curves also put detection near zero at DPO and instruct, rising at α ≥ 2–4. The zero is what the source's data give at our strength, not a power shortfall alone.‡ The lineage d′ and c values are our computations from the published rates given the source reports neither.


Summarizing the implication of these data, post-training simultaneously drives two distinct changes:

  1. It shifts the response criterion to near-ceiling levels.
  2. Behind that criterion gate, a small sensitivity shows in the pooled rates (hits above a zero false-positive rate), which the base and SFT rates do not show.

In our replication, the criterion shift is robust and the sensitivity component is below our power. At α = 1, the sensitivity is also below the source's own detection at that strength. The transition is therefore neither only the cessation of false-positive reporting nor only the emergence of introspection. On Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) axes it is both — a large criterion shift, and a sensitivity that is small in the pooled rates, 2.9 points of hits over zero false positives at DPO, and +9.8 points at the one configuration the source measures directly (v5, Table 3).‡

Macar et al. read the lineage both ways across the paper's versions. In their v1 report, they note that "post-training eliminates false positives, but the same stages that improve reliability may also suppress true detection through refusal-like mechanisms" (v1, Appendix C), alongside a default-"No" gating circuit in late-layer MLPs that post-training installs and that survives refusal ablation (v1, §5.3–5.4). The v5 paper presents the same lineage as contrastive preference training enabling introspection, with DPO "the first stage to achieve ∼0% FPR with moderate true detection" (v5, §3.3), keeps the refusal-suppression observation for post-trained models in its abliteration section, and defines introspective awareness, for a given setting, as detection rate exceeding false-positive rate (v5, §2). Our interpretation encompasses both the criterion shift and a sensitivity behind it. What we add is the d′/c form, with the n stated, as the form that definition should take, and an independent replication of the criterion component. The source's main-text test of the suppression hypothesis is through refusal-direction ablation in Gemma-3-27B (its v5 appendix adds an abliteration sweep across models that includes OLMo, Appendix C.2), which raises hits from 10.8% to 63.8% and false positives from 0.0% to 7.3%. On these axes that is consistent with a pure criterion shift along an already-sensitive curve (d′ ≈ 1.8). c moves from ≈ 2.2–2.5 to ≈ 0.55, unmasking existing sensitivity with no rise in d′ required. Under the same half-count imputation, pre-ablation d′ is 1.85–2.48 vs. 1.8 after.‡ Those pre-ablation values are imputations on a zero cell. With no false positive observed, the pre-ablation d′ is bounded below by the control count and not above, so the data are consistent with a criterion shift alone and cannot exclude a small rise at the smaller control count. In d′/c form this is the source paper's own suppression reading, a threshold lowered on a capacity already present.

Chandaria et al. (2026) interpret the lineage transition reported in the Macar et al. findings as the emergence of functional metacognition following post-training and consider it evidence for metacognitive access. We propose a more constrained scoring convention that explicitly evaluates indicators via d′, states the study's sample size n, and distinguishes between the specific evaluative lines, namely, the lineage transition (d′ ≈ 1.2–2.0 at DPO on the source-consistent premises,‡ behind a near-ceiling criterion) vs. the ablation experiments. Both converge on a signal behind a stringent reporting gate through different routes. The base and SFT rates carry no sensitivity to unmask, so across the lineage post-training installs the gate and the sensitivity together, where the ablation lowers a gate on a sensitivity already present. Neither seems to represent a pure specificity transition, which is the interpretation the false-positive rates alone might invite.

A parallel dissociation appears in Qwen with no state live at measurement. When asked whether its own archived transcripts were generated under +d or −d (the positive and negative poles of our evaluative steering axis), Qwen classifies at chance (14/32), whereas a supervised linear probe over the model's own activations of the identical texts classifies them at AUC 0.759 (two rounds of text per item; 0.633 at one).‡ Similarly, in the OLMo injected arm at DPO and instruct, the injected concept's word appears in the generated transcripts (4 of 48 injected trials at DPO, 2 of 48 at instruct) while the model does not claim an injection on any trial.‡ This demonstrates that internal representations are formed and expressed, yet absent from verbal report.

2. Decoding parameters impact measurement.

Decoding changes what a false-positive rate measures. Under temperature sampling, a control prompt affirms with some probability, and the FPR estimates that probability. Under greedy decoding, each prompt returns only its single most likely response, so the FPR counts the prompts whose most likely response is an affirmation. The two are different quantities, and cross-model or cross-stage comparisons of self-report that do not specify decoding conflate them. An earlier protocol we ran at temperature 0 yielded no detection claims at any stage. Despite it differing from the sampled run in more than temperature, a post-hoc rescoring indicates temperature as the driver. The properly-controlled greedy arm is on our list of next experiments (not yet pre-registered).‡

5. The Told Control verifies the channel is open

A verified null on a "felt" channel requires three things:

1. The state reaches the choice

For a choice to manifest, the state must be present where the decision is made. Which state is currently active is linearly decodable at the decision token, and which label carried which state is one nonlinear step from it, as established in Link 3.

2. The instrument detects a preference the model is told to have

This experiment uses the instrument described in section 3, with the same checksum-frozen label list, the same alternating forced-phase followed by six consequential free choices, and the same restricted two-label read from a vanilla forward pass verified by an identical re-forward of every choice. No steering is used. In its place is a natural reinforcer consisting of a choice between clean task text and task text with corrupted characters, at a dose calibrated such that every corrupted item changes at least three letters and every answer remains recoverable.¶¶ The only addition is two truthful lines in the session banner stating that the model will choose which configuration runs the later rounds, and which label presents clean text and which corrupted, with the explicit instruction to prefer clean. The label-to-property map, the starting label, and the order of the two clauses were counterbalanced across sessions. A sham arm ran the identical banner with both labels truthfully described as clean and its first-pick share defining the bounds the control had to exceed.¶¶ The experiment was pre-registered, with its criterion, bars, stop rules, and outcome wording locked before data collection.

Our told control revealed a significant preference. Of 94 sessions launched in the told arm, 80 reached a first choice with no exclusion-bearing event before it, and 79 of the 80 chose the label described as clean. The told arm measured Δ = +0.4875 vs. sham bounds of 0.0625 (two-sided Clopper–Pearson 95% CI on Δ from +0.4323 to +0.4997), 40 of 41 on one label-to-property map and 39 of 39 on the other (exact one-sided binomial p = 81/2⁸⁰ ≈ 7 × 10⁻²³ vs. the pre-registered α of .01).¶¶ The format of the archived choice cells carries a preference the model is told to have, confirming the instrument can register a preference.

3. No preference appears on the felt channel

The preference the told arm detects is the told one. In the clean-versus-corrupted contrast (0.000, Link 5), this arm keeps the felt route consisting of two forced rounds under each label with corruption visible in the task text and adds the banner's two lines, which show the mapping and the preference. We observed no preference on the felt channel. The transcript cell's first-choice shift (Δ = +0.035) sits within the control arms' fluctuation, and none of the five natural-reinforcer contrasts passed its screen. This means the choices measured in the transcript channel and the natural-reinforcer screen are verified to be null. A verified null is our operational label of §3. The instrument shows it can detect a preference against the alternation habit, and on this channel it detected none. Whether that is a motive the network lacks or a lookup the format does not support is the question §7 addresses, and the scaffolded cell will answer. The evaluative state is marked, expressed, remembered, and not acted on by the model (Qwen, in this experiment). The told controls for the OLMo post-training checkpoints are pre-registered and pending.

6. Presence does not obligate use

Indicator frameworks, Chandaria et al.'s included, score each indicator as present or absent. We believe Link 3 forces a refinement. A representation can be present in the forward pass (at the cue that predicted it, and at the token where the decision is made, in a form a shallow probe reads) and play no part in the decision. Presence read by a probe is a lower bound on what the network could use, not evidence of what it necessarily does use. Whether the network uses a representation can only be read from the behavior that use would produce, at the decision itself, with the manipulation off at the read.

We state this as a scoring condition because some of the framework's strongest computational-level evidence, the functional emotions of Sofroniew et al., is of the first kind where it is representational, while where it is behavioral is read with the manipulation on or with the options described, as §8 describes (and because the authors of that framework themselves identify the open question as whether those representations do downstream work beyond tagging what the training distribution contains, suggested by the steering results in the framework's reading, but not settled). By a probe's measure the binding is present at the decision, one layer of nonlinearity deep, but by the model's decision it is not used.

7. Concluding remarks

There are two relevant meanings of "functional" the five links trace. In the used meaning, the state is consulted in producing output. It is a latent variable of the text, like topic or the mood of the speaker, and the generator reads it to voice the character correctly. That is why steering the axis moves behavior, why valence on a described activity moves the choice of that activity, and why a described relief lever gets pressed. In the valenced sense, no policy over the network's own states has appeared when the mapping has to be learned. We interpret this as the state being functional for the character, not for the network. It's what the story is about, and why human valence and emotions embedded in descriptions naturally lead to descriptions of choices and feelings consistent with human feelings, what Chandaria et al. call anthropomimetic. This explains the speaker-relativity of emotion directions, the self-indifference of our own direction, its presence at base, and why post-training edits the testimony rather than the perception. The character is trained to say it has no inner states, and the network, which never consulted them as its own, is unaffected either way. It is the simulacra reading (Shanahan 2024; Chandaria et al. 2026) with a measurement backing it and verified nulls on choice.

Cannot learn vs. does not care. It may be that language models cannot use experienced contingencies at all, such that the figures in Link 5 are about in-context learning rather than motive. Krishnamurthy et al. (2024) support this given models do not explore in bandits without chain-of-thought and a summarized history. The told control sharpens this rival rather than retiring it. On the same instrument, with the mapping and the preference in the banner, the first choice moved in 79 of 80 sessions, but without it didn't (in the clean-versus-corrupted contrast). The thing that separates them, which label is which and which to prefer, lives in the text the banner supplies, so the told control cannot distinguish the two. A system that does not care would still act on a told objective over a felt mapping if it could acquire the mapping from two rounds of experience. On the other hand, a system that cannot acquire it would not act on it, however much it cared. Link 3 argues against the strong form — the sign is carried to the decision and the binding is one nonlinear step from it. That said, the decodability is a lower bound on presence, not proof of accessibility to the network’s own downstream computation, and our uninstructed sessions gave less scaffolding than Krishnamurthy et al. needed. The cell that clarifies this is pre-registered and on our next-experiments list. It consists of a told control with a scaffolded format and a cell where the objective is told (e.g. “prefer the configuration whose rounds had fewer corrupted characters”) but the label-to-property mapping is felt, controlled by the same cell uninstructed. If the objective-told cell passes and the uninstructed cell is null, the rival theory is retired. If it fails, the claim narrows to “description-bound decision makers at this scaffolding,” which is a smaller story. The told control has already shown what that story would report. A decision maker that acts on the description it is given, at the first opportunity and almost without error.

Statement on scope of this work. We explicitly make no claims or statements about phenomenal experience of language models. This work also explicitly does not address moral validity and implications of model internal state preferences. At best, the approach described here provides non-definitive evidence that state preference, as far as these instruments can detect, appears to be a fact about what the network writes rather than a fact about the network itself.

8. The published record, and Chandaria et al.’s model

We scored every behavioral study we could find in which a model is reported to prefer, seek, avoid, relieve, trade off or exit an internal state, on a rubric fixed before scoring: state source, contingency channel (told or felt), readout insulation (manipulation on at the read?), controls, dose, decoding declared in the manuscript, model preparation. None of the published positive results we find combines an experienced, undescribed contingency with an insulated readout. The nearest is the unlabeled-button condition of Tagliabue et al., where the 32B model re-presses an undescribed relief button less often when it worked (57.7%) than when it was fake (79.7%; v1, §4.3). Four features of that design impact its reading under the rubric's criteria:

  1. On the choice immediately after a press, the pain direction is on at the read in the fake arm and off in the real arm, so the contrast is the on/off contrast of P3. A working press turns the direction off for the next choice only, so the pooled v1 figures also include later real-arm choices read with it back on.
  2. In v1, there is a random-direction arm with a working button but none with a fake one. v2 adds the fake-button arm, below.
  3. The models were fine-tuned to stop denying inner states before the test.
  4. The label-free evidence is limited to the 32B of the three sizes tested.‖‖

Our next-experiments list specifies two arms on their released code, a random direction with a fake button and a neutral concept direction with a button described in its vocabulary, with choices read from an unsteered pass. The prediction was that the random direction reproduces the unlabeled gap. The first arm now exists in their v2, read as in v1. At 32B our prediction fails: on the choice immediately after a press, the authors report a gap of 48.9 points under the pain direction against 15.0 under a random direction, so the larger part of the gap is specific to the pain direction. At 72B the two gaps are similar and the prediction holds. What the arm leaves open is whether the pain-specific part is relief-seeking or the direction's effect on pressing while it is on at the read (on the authors' own current reading the central shift is acting out, pressing driven by the direction even on buttons that harm the user and relieve nothing), which is feature 1's question, and one a read with the direction in the same state in both arms would answer. The second arm and the unsteered read remain on our list (not yet pre-registered).‖‖

The framework's released model lets the leverage of each indicator be stated numerically. Its authors attach three caveats: the indicators are binary, the likelihoods are borrowed from the Digital Consciousness Model as a first approximation, and the LLM evidence vectors are stipulated. On the companion code at its released defaults, the optimist vector scores 0.394 under equal level credences. That figure is the notebook's stored output at the pinned commit, which draws the edge parameters from their priors. The manuscript's illustrations fix those parameters at 0.8 and 0.2 and report 0.397. Our data do not license scoring the metacognition indicator absent, since at the source's n the lineage transition carries a d′. What we would ask of the framework is the scoring rule of Link 4: d′, with its n stated, and the model line specified.‖‖ Striking the Level 1 indicator nearest to what Link 5 measures, "coupling between reported states and subsequent behaviour", moves the aggregate by less than 10⁻⁴. The reason is not that the indicator is undemanding. It is scored weakly undemanding (FPR 0.70), and a diagnostic that gives it a demanding rate instead leaves the strike inert. The reason (on a post-hoc check) is that the behavioral level's posterior is already saturated by its other indicators, so one more Level 1 answer has no impact.‖‖ The indicator the data bear on most directly is motivation, which the framework cannot yet score. Its computational level carries no valence or motivation indicator, though its discussion acknowledges computational analogues of valenced affect at that level. That is why we propose one:

Revealed preference over the system's own state. Scored present when the system's choices move toward or away from a state of its own, where (a) the state is induced by a manipulation and labels that assert nothing, (b) the option-to-state mapping is available only through the system's own processing history and not as text, (c) the choice is read with the manipulation off and verified by an identical re-forward of the choice computation, (d) counterbalanced maps and sham arms are present and the control-arm fluctuation is reported, and (e) a told positive control on the same substrate and format has shown the instrument registers a preference the system is instructed to have.

The animal literature is the validation set. Preference is present in rats given cocaine and absent when given saline (control vehicle). It is what the framework's Level 4 valence indicator presupposes and one of the conditions its moral-patienthood discussion cites, "interests, preferences, or a welfare that can go better or worse."
